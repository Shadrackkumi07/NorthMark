Janitorial pricing per square foot is a shorthand that divides a monthly or annual cleaning price by the building's cleanable area. It is useful for rough budgeting and for tracking one building's cost over time, but it is a poor way to compare bids or buildings, because the real cost driver is labor hours, which depend on frequency, restrooms, floor types, occupancy and scope. The reliable approach is to convert any bid into weekly labor hours and compare those.

## Where per-square-foot pricing comes from

Cleaning companies estimate work using production rates: the square feet a trained cleaner can service per hour for a particular space type and task list. Multiply cleanable area by frequency, divide by production rate and you get labor hours. Multiply hours by a loaded billing rate and you get a price. Dividing that price back by square footage produces the per-square-foot figure buyers often hear.

So a per-square-foot price is not a starting point. It is a result. It hides the assumptions underneath it.

## Why per-square-foot comparisons mislead

### Different buildings need different labor per square foot

| Building | Why its labor per square foot differs |
|---|---|
| Open-plan office, mostly carpet, few restrooms | Fast production; relatively low labor per square foot |
| Medical clinic, many small rooms, hard floors, many restrooms | Slow production; high labor per square foot |
| Warehouse with small office wing | Office and restrooms drive labor; floor area may barely count |
| Bank branch | Small area but detailed glass and lobby work, security procedures |
| School or childcare center | Many restrooms and touchpoints, heavy use |

A low per-square-foot number for a warehouse and a high one for a clinic can both be fair.

### Different bids assume different scopes

Two bids for the same building can differ because one includes weekly desk dusting, monthly high dusting and breakroom appliance interiors, while the other does not. Same building, same per-square-foot label, different work.

### Square footage itself varies

Is it gross area, rentable area or cleanable area? Do stairwells, mechanical rooms and storage count? BOMA International publishes floor measurement standards used for leasing, but cleaning bids often use whatever number the buyer provides. A bid "per square foot" on 12,000 gross square feet and another on 9,500 cleanable square feet are not comparable.

### Frequency is buried

A price per square foot per month includes frequency. Five nights versus three changes the number dramatically, and bids sometimes assume different frequencies than the buyer intended.

## The better method: compare labor hours

Ask every bidder: **how many labor hours per week does this price assume?** Then do the math:

1. **Monthly price ÷ 4.33 = weekly price.**
2. **Weekly price ÷ weekly labor hours = implied hourly rate.**
3. Compare **hours** first. Is each bidder planning enough time for your scope?
4. Compare **implied hourly rates** second. Do they plausibly cover wages, payroll costs, supervision, supplies, insurance and overhead?

In the Fargo metro, BLS reports a mean wage of $20.11 an hour for building and grounds cleaning and maintenance occupations. A bid whose implied hourly rate is close to, or below, the wage alone cannot be covering payroll taxes, insurance, supplies and supervision. Something will give, usually the hours actually worked.

## Illustrative comparison

> Hypothetical figures for explanation only.

| | Bid A | Bid B | Bid C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly price | $1,400 | $1,950 | $2,600 |
| Stated weekly hours | 8 | 11 | 12 |
| Implied hourly rate | $40 | $41 | $50 |
| Scope matches request? | Missing weekly detail tasks | Yes | Yes, plus extras not requested |

Bid A looks cheapest per square foot, but it plans fewer hours and drops tasks. Bid C includes extras you may not need. Bid B matches the scope at a plausible rate. Without converting to hours, Bid A would look like the obvious choice.

## When per-square-foot is useful

- **Budgeting a new building** before getting bids
- **Tracking one building's cost** year over year
- **Portfolio benchmarking** across similar buildings with similar scopes
- **Quick sanity checks** to spot a bid wildly out of line

## How to get comparable bids

1. Give every bidder the same written scope. Use our [janitorial scope of work template](/resources/janitorial-scope-of-work-template).
2. State the cleanable square footage and how you measured it.
3. Specify frequency for each area.
4. Require bidders to state weekly labor hours and staffing.
5. Insist on a [facility walkthrough](/facility-walkthrough).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a typical janitorial price per square foot?

It varies too much by building type, frequency and scope to give a meaningful single number. Compare labor hours and scope instead.

### Why do clinics cost more per square foot than offices?

Clinics have many small rooms, more restrooms, hard floors and more detailed tasks, so they take more labor per square foot.

### Should cleaning be priced on gross or cleanable square footage?

Cleanable square footage is more accurate, but whichever you use, give every bidder the same number and definition.

### How do I spot an unrealistic bid?

Divide the weekly price by stated weekly hours. If the implied hourly rate barely covers local wages, the bid is unlikely to sustain the scope.
