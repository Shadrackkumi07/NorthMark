OSHA's general industry standards shape warehouse housekeeping in three main ways: walking-working surfaces must be kept clean, orderly and as free of hazards as practical (29 CFR 1910.22); exit routes must stay unobstructed (1910.37); and toilet, washing and eating facilities must be sanitary (1910.141). Good housekeeping also reduces fire, forklift and stacking hazards that OSHA's warehousing guidance highlights. Most warehouses split the work: operations staff handle floor-level housekeeping in active areas, while a janitorial vendor cleans restrooms, breakrooms, offices and designated walkways. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

## The key standards

### Walking-working surfaces (1910.22)

The standard requires that all places of employment, passageways, storerooms and service rooms be kept clean, orderly and sanitary, and that walking-working surfaces be maintained free of hazards such as sharp or protruding objects, loose boards, corrosion, leaks, spills, snow and ice, as far as practical. Floors should be kept dry where practical, or drainage, mats or other measures provided where wet processes are used.

**Practical meaning:** spills cleaned promptly, debris removed from aisles, wet entries managed, and damaged floor surfaces reported and repaired.

### Exit routes (1910.37)

Exit routes must be kept free and unobstructed by materials, equipment or decorations, and exit signs must be visible and lit as required.

**Practical meaning:** no pallets, carts or overflow storage in exit paths, and exits checked regularly.

### Sanitation (1910.141)

The sanitation standard covers toilet facilities, washing facilities, waste disposal and, where employees eat on site, food consumption areas.

**Practical meaning:** restrooms clean and supplied, waste removed regularly, breakrooms kept sanitary.

## Why housekeeping prevents injuries

OSHA's warehousing page identifies hazards including forklifts, docks, conveyors, material storage, manual lifting and slips, trips and falls. Housekeeping touches many of them:

| Hazard | Housekeeping connection |
|---|---|
| Slips, trips and falls | Spills, debris, stretch wrap, banding and wet entries |
| Forklift incidents | Debris in travel lanes, blocked visibility at intersections |
| Fire | Accumulated cardboard, dust and packaging |
| Blocked egress | Overflow storage in exit routes |
| Struck-by | Unstable or poorly stacked materials |

## Who does what

A common and effective split:

| Area or task | Operations staff | Janitorial vendor |
|---|---|---|
| Spill response in active areas | Yes, immediately | — |
| Removing stretch wrap, banding, pallet debris in aisles | Yes, continuously | Designated sweeping if scoped |
| Racking and storage areas | Yes | — |
| Exit route clearance | Yes (daily responsibility) | Reports obstructions |
| Designated walkways and staging lanes | Shared | Sweeping or dust-mopping as scoped |
| Restrooms and locker rooms | — | Yes |
| Breakrooms and driver lounges | — | Yes |
| Office wing | — | Yes |
| Entries and mats | Shared in winter | Yes |

The key principle: anything inside active operations, around equipment or under time pressure stays with trained operations staff. The janitorial vendor handles the people spaces and designated areas, on a schedule, and reports hazards it sees.

## A simple housekeeping routine

**Every shift (operations):** clear aisle debris, respond to spills, keep exits clear, collect stretch wrap and banding.

**Daily (janitorial):** restrooms, locker rooms, breakrooms, driver lounges, office wing, entries; sweep designated walkways.

**Weekly (both):** walk exit routes, check mats and drainage at docks, review problem areas.

**Monthly:** housekeeping inspection by a supervisor or safety lead, with findings tracked to closure.

Snow and ice at dock doors and entrances are specifically named among walking-working surface hazards. Fargo–Moorhead warehouses need winter mat plans, more frequent entry attention and prompt reporting of ice. See [winter facility care in Fargo](/resources/winter-facility-care-fargo).

## Choosing a janitorial vendor for a warehouse

Ask whether the vendor:

- Completes your site safety orientation
- Follows your PPE and walkway rules
- Stays out of active equipment zones
- Reports hazards immediately
- Schedules around shifts

## Documenting housekeeping

Keeping simple records helps both safety and accountability: a dated monthly housekeeping inspection with photos of findings, the janitorial vendor's visit logs and checklists, and a record of how each finding was corrected. If an incident occurs, those records show that housekeeping was managed deliberately, and they reveal patterns that point to lasting fixes.

## Frequently asked questions

### What OSHA standard covers warehouse housekeeping?

The walking-working surfaces standard, 29 CFR 1910.22, is central, along with exit routes (1910.37) and sanitation (1910.141).

### Can a janitorial company handle all warehouse housekeeping?

Usually not. Active-area housekeeping stays with operations staff. Janitorial vendors clean people spaces and designated walkways.

### How often should warehouse restrooms be cleaned?

At least daily in most operations, often after shift changes in multi-shift facilities.

### Is this guide legal advice?

No. It summarizes general requirements. Consult OSHA resources or a safety professional for your situation.
