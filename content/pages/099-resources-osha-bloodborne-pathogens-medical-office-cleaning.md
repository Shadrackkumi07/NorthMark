OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030, requires employers whose workers can reasonably be expected to contact blood or other potentially infectious materials to protect them through an exposure control plan, engineering and work practice controls, personal protective equipment, hepatitis B vaccination offers, training and housekeeping rules, including a written cleaning schedule and safe handling of regulated waste and contaminated sharps. For medical offices, the standard is a major reason to define clearly which cleaning tasks belong to trained clinical staff and which belong to a janitorial vendor. This guide is general information, not legal or compliance advice.

## Who the standard covers

The standard applies to employers with employees who have **occupational exposure**, meaning reasonably anticipated contact with blood or other potentially infectious materials as a result of their duties. In a medical office, clinical staff clearly have exposure. Whether janitorial staff do depends on what they are assigned to do. If a cleaning vendor's employees are assigned tasks with reasonably anticipated exposure, their employer has obligations under the standard for them.

That is the practical heart of the matter: **the scope decides the exposure**.

## Key requirements

| Requirement | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Exposure control plan | Written plan identifying exposed job classifications and tasks, reviewed and updated at least annually |
| Engineering and work practice controls | Sharps containers, safer devices, hand hygiene, procedures that minimize exposure |
| Personal protective equipment | Gloves, gowns, eye and face protection as appropriate, provided at no cost |
| Housekeeping | Written schedule for cleaning and decontamination; decontaminating contaminated surfaces; handling broken glass with mechanical means |
| Regulated waste | Proper containers, labeling and disposal |
| Hepatitis B vaccination | Offered to exposed employees, generally within 10 working days of assignment |
| Post-exposure evaluation | Medical evaluation and follow-up after an exposure incident |
| Training | At assignment and annually |
| Recordkeeping | Training and medical records as required |

## Housekeeping provisions in practice

The standard's housekeeping section requires the employer to keep the worksite clean and sanitary, implement a written schedule for cleaning and decontamination based on location, surfaces and tasks, decontaminate equipment and surfaces after contact with blood, and handle regulated waste and contaminated sharps properly. It also directs that broken glassware that may be contaminated be picked up with mechanical means such as a brush and dustpan or tongs, never by hand.

## Dividing work between clinical and janitorial staff

Most medical offices follow a division like this:

| Task | Typically handled by |
|---|---|
| Decontaminating surfaces after blood or body fluid contact | Trained clinical staff under the exposure control plan |
| Exam tables and treatment surfaces between patients | Clinical staff |
| Sharps containers | Clinical staff and licensed waste hauler |
| Regulated (red-bag) waste | Clinical staff and licensed waste hauler |
| Routine floor, restroom, waiting room and office cleaning | Janitorial vendor |
| Non-regulated trash | Janitorial vendor |
| Designated exam room surfaces such as floors, sinks and handles | Janitorial vendor, as agreed |

This division keeps janitorial staff out of tasks with reasonably anticipated exposure, while ensuring the facility is cleaned. It must be written into the scope.

## What janitorial staff should do if they encounter blood or sharps

Even with clear boundaries, a cleaner may find a stray needle or a blood spill. The vendor and practice should agree a simple rule:

1. **Stop. Do not touch.**
2. Keep others away from the area.
3. Report to the practice's designated contact per the agreed escalation procedure.
4. Let trained clinical staff handle cleanup under the exposure control plan.

If an exposure incident occurs, the employer's post-exposure procedures apply.

## Products

The CDC's environmental infection control guidelines describe matching cleaning and disinfection to the area and risk. Products used by janitorial staff in patient areas should be agreed with the practice, EPA-registered where disinfection is intended and used per label directions. See [cleaning vs sanitizing vs disinfecting](/resources/cleaning-vs-sanitizing-vs-disinfecting).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard apply to janitors?

It applies to employees with occupational exposure. Whether a janitorial employee has exposure depends on assigned tasks. Clear scopes that exclude regulated waste, sharps and blood cleanup reduce exposure.

### Should cleaning staff handle sharps containers?

Generally no. Sharps are handled by trained clinical staff and licensed waste haulers.

### What should a cleaner do if they find a needle?

Stop, do not touch it, keep others away and report it to the practice's designated contact.

### Is this guide compliance advice?

No. It summarizes the standard generally. Consult OSHA resources or a qualified professional for your situation.
